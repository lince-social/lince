Chapter 7 — Automation

Karma

Everything up to here you do by hand. Karma is how Lince does things without you. And it is one sentence long: 

graph LR
  A["If: Condition"] --> B["And: Threshold"] --> C["Then: Consequence"]

In ordinary language:

graph LR
  A["If: tomorrow is cold"] --> B["And: cold enough to matter"] --> C["Then: need to pack a sweater"]

And when the world says otherwise, the same rule simply does nothing:

graph LR
  A["If: tomorrow is mild"] --> B["And: not cold enough"] --> C["Then: nothing happens"]

"If" is the Condition

The condition is an equation, and it can draw on three kinds of input:

graph LR
  A["A Frequency"] --> Z["Condition"]
  B["A shell command"] --> Z
  C["A record's quantity"] --> Z

Each contributes a number, and the ranges matter more than they look:

  • A Frequency gives 0 or 1 — it is the clock. It sits on a Cadence ("monthly until June", "every 14th"), and stays 0 until the scheduled instant arrives.
  • A command gives whatever it prints, read as a number.
  • A record's quantity gives anything at all, from very negative to very positive.

So a condition is arithmetic over live data. Write it abstractly and then substitute what is actually true right now: 

graph TD
  A["Condition: Data > 5"] --> B["Condition: that record's quantity > 5"]
  B --> C["Condition: 2 > 5"]
  C --> D["Condition: false"]

The same rule, resolved one step at a time against the world as it is.

"And" is the Threshold

The threshold is the guard between having a number and doing something about it. The usual one is simply "not zero". 

graph LR
  A["Condition: 0 (false)"] --> B["Threshold: not 0"] --> C["stops here"]
graph LR
  A["Condition: 2 (a quantity)"] --> B["Threshold: not 0"] --> C["Consequence runs"]

This is why a Frequency returning 0 is enough to keep a whole rule quiet.

"Then" is the Consequence

graph LR
  A["Consequence"] --> B["Change a record's quantity"]
  A --> C["Run a shell command"]
  A --> D["Run SQL"]

The first one is the interesting one, because a changed quantity is itself something another rule can be watching. Rules chain without ever referring to each other: 

graph TD
  A["Condition: record reaches a value"] --> B["Consequence: another record changes"]
  B --> C["Condition: that record changed"]
  C --> D["Consequence: a command runs"]

Nobody wired these two rules together. They meet in the data.

A monthly example

graph LR
  A["-1 * (Frequency * record quantity)"] --> B["Threshold"] --> C["another record's quantity"]

For most of the month the Frequency is 0, so the whole equation is 0, so the threshold stops it and nothing happens. On the scheduled day the Frequency becomes 1, the equation takes the value of the first record, and that amount moves into the second. A monthly bill, with no scheduler and no special "recurring" feature — just a number that is zero most of the time.

The whole picture

graph LR
  A["Frequency"] --> B["Condition"]
  C["Command"] --> B
  D["SQL"] --> B
  E["Record"] --> B
  B --> F["Threshold"]
  F --> G["Record"]
  F --> H["Command"]
  F --> I["SQL"]

The same kinds of thing go in and come out — which is exactly why rules compose.

Notice what is missing: there is no list of automations Lince supports. There is one shape — if, and, then — pointed at records you already have. A budget that refills, a habit that nags, a stock level that reorders, and a Transfer that settles itself are the same machine with different numbers in it.
